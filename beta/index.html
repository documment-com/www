The problem

Paper

Airframe, engine, propeller and avionics logbooks spanning decades and several owners, in a flight bag or a filing cabinet.

Sticky notes

Oil changes, the annual, ELT, transponder and pitot-static checks tracked in a spreadsheet, or in the shop's memory.

Lock-in

Records tools that keep your data when you switch products or sell the aircraft. A lost logbook can take a real bite out of resale value.

How it works

From a box of logbooks to a current due list.

  1. 1

    Scan your logbooks

    Photograph pages with the iPad or iPhone, or upload PDFs. Each page is read on M2's server by AI, which proposes every entry with the page reference it came from.

  2. 2

    Check them side by side

    You, or your mechanic, review each entry next to the page: split, join, correct, or add one it missed. Nothing counts until a person confirms it, and the original scan is always kept.

  3. 3

    Set the times

    Enter tach and Hobbs. M2 builds the due list (annual, oil, ELT, transponder, pitot-static, RTBO) and catches last-done dates up from your own records.

  4. 4

    Invite your mechanic

    Share the aircraft with your A&P or IA. Their account is free. They write entries on the iPad and sign by hand; you apply them to the logbook.

Features

Paper in. Checked records out.

Each scanned page sits beside what was read from it. Every entry is outlined on the page, so you can check the transcription line by line against the original handwriting or type.

Confirm it, correct it, or mark an entry the reader missed by tapping its lines. The scan is kept as the original record; confirmed entries are permanent, and a mistake is fixed with a correcting entry.

Know what's due, honestly.

Every due item shows both limits: tach 3,310.0 or Feb 20, 2027, whichever first. Total times for airframe and engine, time since major and top overhaul, and the next thing coming up, at a glance.

M2 says No overdue items on record. It never says "airworthy" or "legal to fly": airworthiness is determined by you and your IA.

Do your own preventive maintenance, by the book.

M2 checks that the aircraft is flown under Part 91 and that your pilot certificate is on your profile, then lists the 31 preventive maintenance tasks of 14 CFR Part 43 Appendix A(c) that an owner-pilot may do.

Pick a task, or search for what you did, and log it with its reference. You sign the entry with your finger or Apple Pencil.

ADs matched to your aircraft.

M2 matches airworthiness directives published since 1994 to your airframe, engine, propeller and installed equipment, says why each one was flagged, and asks about equipment an AD depends on, such as a magneto or ELT.

M2 suggests; a mechanic decides. Each AD links to the signed entry that complied with it, or the signed not-applicable determination.

AI-enabled records, manuals, and documents.

Upload your maintenance manuals, parts catalogs and service letters; they stay in your account and become searchable. A shared library keeps the ADs and 14 CFR Parts 43 and 91 current for everyone.

Ask M2 answers questions like "When was the last annual?" from your records and documents only, and shows every source it used.

Signed entries

Signed by hand. Sealed for good.

By hand, on the device

Everyone who makes an entry signs it with a finger, Apple Pencil or stylus, on iPad, iPhone or a touch-screen browser. An entry can't reach the logbook unsigned.

Sealed and time-stamped

The entry, the signature, the signer's name and certificate number are hashed, time-stamped (RFC 3161) and made immutable. Corrections are new entries, in line with FAA AC 120-78B.

Documents kept with the entry

8130-3s, yellow tags, Form 337s, invoices and photos attach to the entry they support, and the seal covers them.

The owner applies

A mechanic's signed entry comes to you. Apply it to the logbook, or send it back with a comment. Only the author edits an entry.

On iPhone

The owner-pilot's app, in your flight bag.

Log tach and Hobbs after a flight, write up a squawk, record an oil change, or ask a question about your aircraft. Ask M2 runs on the iPhone and iPad with Apple's on-device model.

FAQ

Does M2 tell me my aircraft is airworthy?

No. M2 tells you what's on record, and whether anything on record is overdue. Airworthiness is determined by you and your mechanic or IA. M2's AI drafts, finds and cites; a person decides and signs.

How are my paper logbooks read?

Pages you scan are read on M2's server by AI, which proposes each entry and the part of the page it came from. You or your mechanic check each one against the page before it's confirmed. The scan itself is never altered and is kept as the record of what the paper said.

Where does Ask M2 run?

On your iPad or iPhone, with Apple's on-device model. Answers come only from your records and documents, with their sources listed.

Does my mechanic need to pay?

No. A&Ps and IAs who work on aircraft shared with them have free accounts, with no limit on invitations. A mechanic who wants to manage their own aircraft chooses a plan like any owner.

Which aircraft and countries are supported?

The private beta is for US-registered aircraft operated under Part 91, including experimental and light-sport aircraft with condition inspections. Canada is next; the data model is built for other registries later.

What happens to my records if I leave?

Close your account or remove an aircraft and the records become read-only for 30 days, during which you can download the full export package, free. Sold the aircraft to someone on M2? Transfer the record to them instead.

Which devices does M2 work on?

iPad and the web for the full app, including mechanic signing; iPhone for the owner-pilot's everyday tasks. The iPad works offline for reading records and quick entries and syncs later.
